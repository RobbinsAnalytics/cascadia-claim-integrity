Picture the service

A ride to a medical appointment.

  1. A patient needs help reaching a scheduled medical appointment.
  2. A covered benefit arranges an appropriate ride, such as an authorized Uber or Lyft trip.
  3. The patient arrives. The billing and reporting records still need support.

Illustrative example, not a patient or trip in the dataset.

01 / Why this matters

A completed ride does not guarantee payment.

Non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT) helps eligible people reach medical care when they need transportation assistance. A scheduled ride to a medical appointment is one example.

The financial consequence

The service must be supported before it can be reimbursed.

For a covered ride, missing required documentation can delay or prevent reimbursement. Completing the trip alone does not establish a payable claim.

  • Who: identify the member and the billing provider.
  • What happened: document the service, date, locations and supported quantity.
  • What is required: meet the program’s coverage, authorization and billing rules.
Source: CMS NEMT provider guidance, documentation and billing, pages 10–11 ↗
Which programs?

The people and organizations

Who is the client, and who does what?

In this model, the client is a health plan. It hires and pays a transportation broker to manage rides for its members. The patient receives the service; a ride company performs the transportation.

This example covers Medicaid and Medicare Advantage transportation benefits. A claim, a payment and an encounter reporting obligation remain distinct.

The missing connection

Where do the records come from?

The ride company sends a trip record, not a complete healthcare claim. The NEMT team combines it with the health plan’s member and benefit information and its own billing and payment records.

Why not one complete record from every company?

  • Different jobs: a ride receipt records a trip; a claim also has to satisfy healthcare billing requirements.
  • Different rules: state, plan and contract requirements vary. The same trip record may support one use and need more information for another.
  • Different handoffs: supporting references and responses can arrive separately or through another organization.

The modeled gap: some operators already integrate these records. This build addresses the situation where the complete, supported claim record is not available.

Background: CMS describes separate documentation responsibilities and varying state and broker requirements ↗

Start here / Three terms

From a completed ride to a usable record

Ride record

What service happened?

The record of a transportation service. It may lack information needed for billing or reporting.

Claim

What supports payment?

A structured record supporting a request for payment. This build assembles claim facts from ride records and supporting evidence.

Encounter

What must be reported?

A record of a service used for health plan or state reporting. This build models that reporting downstream of claim preparation.

A recipient can accept a submission without establishing payment or proving every fact correct. These are model explanations, not a claim of actual payer submission or standards certification.

04 / Decisions the model makes possible

Explain what needs attention, and why.

01

Keep unfinished claims in the workload.

What: count expected work from the source service population, including components whose claim was never built.

Example: ten rides require twenty components. If two components are missing, the result is 18 of 20, not 18 of 18.

Why: the operations team can see the two missing components and investigate them. Excluding them would hide work and inflate completion.

02

Separate a missing fact from a later correction.

What: retain when the service happened and when supporting evidence became available.

Example: the billing identifier received July 16 cannot make the July 14 claim ready.

Why: a manager can distinguish a late evidence handoff from a team failing to use information it already had.

03

Find which handoff changed the facts.

What: compare source records → assembled claim → sent content as separate steps.

Illustration: the ride record says 4.11 miles. If the claim says 41.1, inspect claim construction. If the claim says 4.11 but the submission says 41.1, inspect submission mapping.

Why: the two errors need different owners and fixes. A recipient’s acceptance alone would not identify either one.

04

Send the follow-up to the right place.

What: preserve missing-payment evidence, missing responses and confirmed rejections as different conditions.

Example: seek a vendor-payment record from the payment-data handoff; seek a rejection explanation from the recipient’s response route.

Why: teams can investigate the actual dependency instead of repeatedly rebuilding a claim that is already supported.

The ten-ride and decimal-error examples illustrate the method; they are not additional measured results. Suggested follow-up is not evidence that outreach occurred.

05 / How the model supports those decisions

Follow one claim through the controls.

Implementation details

This model preserves source records, claim versions, target requirements, submitted fields and response links in Python and JSON/JSONL. The browser presents a frozen analytical bundle using HTML, CSS, JavaScript and SVG.

The sent record is a synthetic payload, not a certified healthcare EDI transaction. EDI is the standard electronic exchange format used by many healthcare billing systems; transaction compliance is outside this build.

Inspect the numerical comparison ↗

These are values, not claims.

A published numerical cell is one measure value for a defined client filter, period, cutoff position and, where relevant, stage.

Example: 21,356 ready components at the full-scenario July 21 position is one value. The corresponding denominator, 24,040, is another. Counts for different clients and dates are separate values.

Business value: recounting them from the sources helps detect missing work, double counting or use of the wrong cutoff before a manager uses the dashboard to prioritize a queue. Agreement supports the calculation, not real-world savings or every modeling assumption.

Business rules were checked with known-answer examples.

  • Missing is not zero: absent payment evidence must not become a recorded $0 payment or silently clear a required evidence hold.
  • A correction has a date: the July 16 billing reference must not make the July 14 record ready.
  • Accepted is not necessarily correct: compare the submitted fields with the supported claim even when a receiver accepts the message.

A check on evidence meaning

An acceptance without a decision time must stay visible.

The response exists, but its supported decision time does not. The model keeps that acceptance candidate visible without giving it accepted-coverage credit. A recorded check verifies that dropping the candidate triggers the control.

Business reason: preserve the item for follow-up instead of hiding it or claiming a timely acceptance that the evidence cannot support.

What was tested, and what does “152 checks pass” mean?

The recorded prepublication checklist contains 152 passing entries and 6 N/A dispositions. These are control and diagnostic observations, not 152 claims or a business success rate. N/A items are not counted as passes.

  • Model checks: known-answer scenarios and deliberate faults test rules such as preventing duplicate effects and preserving earlier cutoffs.
  • Presentation checks: recorded browser checks test filters, exact chart readouts and exports. They help the reader reach and inspect the intended records; they do not establish healthcare expertise or business impact.
  • Examples of review fixes: missing vendor-payment evidence gained a concrete ledger example; unresolved responses gained a recipient-specific follow-up explanation. The review was simulated and restricted, not human user research.

07 / Next business questions

Test the next questions with better evidence.

The next iteration could test these four business questions. Real partner data would be needed to estimate how often the modeled problems occur.

Payment evidence

Which records would release the most blocked work?

Decision
Prioritize the payment-data handoff or another missing-data dependency.
Why it matters
If payment evidence clears many holds, obtaining that feed may be more useful than repeatedly rebuilding claims. Ready does not mean paid.
Next experiment
Add later payment evidence to matched scenarios and measure which components become ready, keeping other facts fixed.

Partner visibility

Is the service outcome missing, or just its response?

Decision
Improve the response feed or investigate actual rejected submissions.
Why it matters
A visibility problem needs better return evidence. A confirmed rejection needs the recipient’s explanation and an appropriate correction.
Next experiment
Vary response routes while holding the underlying synthetic decision fixed.

Work prioritization

Which follow-up should come first?

Decision
Assign limited staff time to the most urgent, actionable queue.
Why it matters
Deadline risk, dependency and expected effort can change which follow-up is worth doing first. A large queue alone does not establish priority.
Next experiment
Compare explicit deadline and effort assumptions, then validate response times before estimating benefits.

Operational realism

Would real partner data change the priority?

Decision
Choose which integration or process improvement deserves investment.
Why it matters
Deliberately balanced test cases cannot reveal which problems dominate an actual operation. Staffing or savings estimates need observed volumes and timing.
Next experiment
Calibrate the scenario mix with suitable partner data and compare the ranking of proposed improvements.
